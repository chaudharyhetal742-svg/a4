Aviation History & Ergonomics

Shearling Collar and Storm Latch Engineering in Flight Jackets

By Julian Vance • September 2026 • 10 Min Read • Verified Technical Archive

1. Open-Cockpit Vulnerability: The Suprathyroid Vascular Complex

In the early decades of military aviation, open cockpits exposed pilots directly to severe wind chill. At an airspeed of 120 knots and an altitude of 10,000 feet, ambient temperatures routinely dropped below -20°C (-4°F). Under these extreme conditions, the human neck represents an acute thermal vulnerability. The carotid arteries and internal jugular veins pass within millimeters of the skin surface along the anterior cervical triangle. Exposure of this vascular junction to freezing air triggers rapid systemic cooling, peripheral vasoconstriction, cognitive fog, and physical shivering.

Military tailoring quickly recognized that standard turnover collars were woefully inadequate. Wind currents forced their way between the lapels, circulating freezing air down the spine. The solution was the shearling storm collar—a high-rising protective barrier crafted from un-shorn sheepskin pelt with the natural wool fleece facing inward against the neck.

2. The Metallurgy and Mechanics of the Dual-Buckle Throat Latch

A storm collar is only as effective as its closure mechanism. In early flight jackets, buttons were frequently ripped off by high-velocity wind or proved impossible to fasten with thick leather flight mittens. Aviation tailors developed the dual-buckle throat latch: an adjustable bridle leather strap riveted to the collar point and fastened through solid brass roller buckles.

The throat latch allows an aviator to cinch the collar firmly around the neck, sealing the opening completely. When flying in milder weather, the latch can be unbuckled and snapped back against the underside of the lapel, allowing the collar to lie flat in a traditional notched formation.

3. Australian Merino Shearling: Pelt Selection and Fiber Crimp

Not all shearling is suitable for flight collar construction. Coarse domestic sheep pelts often feature straight, brittle fibers that feel scratchy against the skin and mat down permanently after exposure to snow and rain. CorduroyFlight exclusively selects premium Australian merino shearling pelts with a uniform 15 to 20 millimeter wool depth.

Merino wool fibers possess an extraordinary three-dimensional crimp—over 25 microscopic waves per inch. This dense fiber matrix creates millions of microscopic dead-air cells that insulate the neck while maintaining velvet softness against sensitive cervical skin. Furthermore, natural lanolin wax present within the wool fibers repels moisture and prevents the collar from becoming damp in fog or driving sleet.

4. Seam Construction: Taped Leather Welts and Heavy Lockstitching

Attaching a dense shearling pelt to a heavy 8-wale corduroy jacket shell presents immense tailoring challenges. Standard flat-felled seams create excessive bulk that chafes the neck. Our master tailors employ specialized furriers' strobel machines to join the shearling collar to the corduroy neckline, finishing the exterior seam with a topstitched bridle leather welt tape.

This reinforced leather tape distributes mechanical tension evenly along the entire neck seam, preventing thread tear-out when the collar is pulled upward against strong winds. Each seam is secured with bonded nylon thread with a tensile strength exceeding 35 pounds per single strand.

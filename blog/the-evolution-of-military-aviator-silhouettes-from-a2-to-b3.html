The Evolution of Military Aviator Silhouettes: From A-2 to B-3

By Arthur Sterling • September 2026 • 10 Min Read • Verified Technical Archive

1. The A-2 Type Specification: Standardized Army Air Corps Outerwear

Formally adopted by the United States Army Air Corps on May 9, 1931, the Type A-2 flight jacket remains one of the most celebrated outerwear silhouettes in military history. Engineered to replace the earlier buttoned A-1 jacket, the A-2 introduced groundbreaking design features that defined twentieth-century outerwear: a front zipper closure protected by a windproof storm placket, snap-down collar points to prevent flapping in open cockpits, ribbed wool cuffs and hem to seal in body heat, and twin front cargo pockets with hidden side-entry hand warmers.

While originally issued in horsehide and goatskin leather, experimental interwar production saw squadrons commissioning bespoke jackets in heavy corduroy and jungle cloth for warmer Mediterranean and Pacific theater operations. CorduroyFlight reimagines this iconic A-2 blueprint, maintaining its tailored proportions while executing the shell in rich 8-wale cotton corduroy.

2. The B-3 Heavy Bomber Jacket: High-Altitude Arctic Defense

As aviation technology advanced in the late 1930s, long-range bombers such as the B-17 Flying Fortress began flying missions at altitudes exceeding 25,000 feet. In unpressurized and unheated waist-gunner compartments, temperatures plummeted to -40°C (-40°F). The lightweight A-2 was incapable of keeping crew members alive under these extreme conditions, leading to the creation of the Type B-3 flight jacket in 1934.

The B-3 discarded the trim bomber silhouette in favor of a heavy, broad-shouldered cut built around thick shearling fleece. It featured a high shearling storm collar, twin leather waist adjustment straps, and heavy leather reinforcement bands across the sleeves and shoulders. In our CorduroyFlight B-3 edition, this arctic heritage is preserved with a heavy corduroy exterior and full shearling insulation.

3. The G-1 Naval Aviator Variant: Bi-Swing Freedom and Fur Trim

Developed concurrently by the United States Navy and Bureau of Aeronautics, the G-1 jacket represented the naval counterpart to the Army's A-2. Because carrier-based naval aviators operated in humid, salty marine environments, the Navy specified a mouton sheepskin collar that resisted sea-spray stiffening.

Most importantly, the G-1 introduced the revolutionary bi-swing action back—an articulated pleat system flanking each shoulder blade. In tight fighter cockpits, this mechanical pleat allowed pilots to reach upward and outward to manipulate overhead switches and canopy latches without the jacket binding across the chest. CorduroyFlight integrates this exact bi-swing architecture into all of our corduroy flight editions.
